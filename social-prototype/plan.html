What we are actually trying to do

Lauren bakes on Monday and delivers Tuesday and Friday. The list closes Sunday at nine. Everything about the business is a weekly loop, and Instagram has to sit inside that loop rather than beside it.

So the target is not “grow the account.” The target is three steps:

  1. A stranger in Peoria stops scrolling — because the post is funny, or beautiful, or true.
  2. They tap the bio link and land on the shop.
  3. They order before Sunday at nine.

Everything here is judged against those. A post that gets four hundred likes from people in Ohio has failed. A post that gets nineteen likes and two orders from Vistancia has worked.

The second job, quieter: the account is the front door for the blessing bags and the verse card. Those are the two things nobody else in the granola business has, and they are the reason someone follows an account about oats.

What actually works on Instagram right now

This is the research, compressed. All hundred posts are built on it.

Reach in 2026 comes from three signals, in this order: watch time (did they finish it), sends (did they DM it to someone), and saves. Likes are close to decorative. So every post in the library has one stated purpose — watched, saved, sent on, commented, or clicked — and is shaped around that single job rather than trying to do all five.

The first line does all the work. The hook has to be specific enough to be believed and narrow enough to feel personally addressed. Which is why the hooks read like

“This jar has been to eleven houses.”
“The pickup line is twenty-two minutes long. Ask me how I know.”

…and never “Try our delicious granola.”

  • Specificity is the whole game. Numbers, street names, times of day, the real temperature, the real number of trays. A woman saying “Monday, 5:41 a.m., Peoria is still dark” is not invisible; generic food-brand language is.
  • Carousels earn swipes, and swipes are dwell time. Anything that is a list, a build, a ranking or a how-to is a carousel with the payoff on the last slide.
  • Short loops beat long videos. A six-second reel that loops cleanly finishes; a thirty-second one does not. Sound on — crunch, honey pouring, a jar lid popping.
  • Save-bait has to be explicit. “Save this for the week you forget you have it.”
  • Send-bait has to name a person. Not “share this” but “you know exactly who in your family this is.” Sends are the most weighted signal there is.
  • Hashtags are nearly dead but not free-dead. Five per post, local-weighted.
  • Faith content works when it costs the writer something. A verse with a pretty photo is scrolled past. A verse taped above the counter, with an admission that some weeks it is a job description rather than an encouragement, is not.

The rule that overrides all of it

Nothing invented. No fake reviews, no invented backstory, no number she has not counted. 23 of the 100 posts are marked “needs your detail” — they are frames with her fact missing, and the page will not let her schedule one until it is filled in.

Connecting Instagram

The honest summary: publishing to Instagram from our own software is possible, free, and does not require Meta’s app review — as long as we only ever post to her own account. That last clause is what makes this easy. Building something that posts on behalf of strangers would be a six-week compliance project. We are not doing that.

What Meta requires, once

  1. Her Instagram is a Professional account. Free, a settings toggle.
  2. It is linked to a Facebook Page. The Page can sit empty and unused; it is plumbing, not a second account to run.
  3. A Meta developer app, created once by us, left in Development mode.
  4. Her account added to that app as an Instagram Tester, invite accepted.

Step 4 is the unlock. In Development mode an app can publish to accounts that have a role on it, with no app review and no business verification. Since the only account we will ever post to is hers, we stay in Development mode permanently.

Scheduling: we build the clock, not Instagram

This is the most important decision in the document. Instagram’s API does not reliably offer future-scheduling the way Facebook Pages do — it is undocumented for Instagram in Meta’s own reference, and it definitively does not exist for Stories or Reels.

Every commercial scheduler — Later, Planoly, Buffer — holds the post in its own database and calls the publish API itself at the right second. We do the same.

It is more code than we would like, and it is also more reliable: we control the retries, we control the token refresh, and a post that fails says so on her page instead of vanishing into Meta’s queue. A job runs every five minutes, picks up whatever is due, and publishes it. Nothing is ever published twice — the Instagram post id, once written down, is the receipt.

Two constraints worth knowing now

  • Photos have to live somewhere public. Instagram fetches the file from a URL itself, so a photo on her phone cannot be posted directly. She uploads it to us, we host it, we hand Instagram the link.
  • Stories cannot be scheduled by anybody. So the page treats stories as a checklist for the day — “post a porch shot on the run today” — not as something it publishes.

What we are not going to do

  • No captions written by a machine at post time. Everything is written in advance and read by her. An AI writing unsupervised in her voice, on a faith brand, is a risk with no upside.
  • No auto-DMs, no follow-bots, no engagement automation. Against Meta’s terms and against the brand.

  2. Where photos live. Vercel Blob, alongside the site — my recommendation.
  3. Do we stop after phase 2? Library and shot list only, no Instagram connection. Cheapest path to most of the value and entirely defensible.
  4. Is she already on GoHighLevel? If she needs to be posting this week, GHL will connect Instagram in ten minutes of clicking with no code. Worth doing as a stopgap. It is not the recommendation, because GHL cannot see which flavor just sold out, which verse is going out, or whether she is baking — and that is exactly what makes “fill my week” worth building inside her own page.

Risks, honestly

  • The Facebook Page requirement is where this stalls. Five minutes with someone sitting next to her; hours alone.
  • Token expiry breaks self-built posters more than anything else. Designed for from the start: weekly refresh, loud banner, posts pause rather than failing one by one.
  • Meta changes the API. It happens. The queue is ours, so a breaking change means posts sit unpublished with a clear error rather than disappearing.
  • The real risk is not technical. It is that she does not have the photos. That is why the shot list exists in phase 2, before any of the API work.
